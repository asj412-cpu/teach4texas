Thanksgiving week is the shortest, loudest stretch of the fall semester. Kids are already halfway to grandma's house, the schedule is broken up, and the easy move is a turkey coloring page or a pie-chart worksheet with a pilgrim hat clip-art stamp.

The problem is that Grade 4 STAAR math doesn't take the week off. Fractions with equal denominators, division with remainders that actually mean something, and measurement word problems are all sitting right there — and they happen to fit a Thanksgiving story better than almost any other time of year. Pies get cut into eighths. Guests have to be seated at tables. Cranberry sauce gets measured in cups and the turkey timer runs in minutes.

So the goal for the week is simple: keep the feast energy, and make every minute of it count toward STAAR.

## The three TEKS that fit a Thanksgiving block

Here they are in plain classroom language.

**TEKS 4.3E** is adding and subtracting fractions with equal denominators, using objects and pictorial models that build to the number line. In the room that sounds like: "Jake has 5/8 of a pumpkin pie and Avery brings 2/8 more — how much pie is on the table?" The trap kids fall into is adding the denominators too and announcing 7/16. A pie model on the page catches that fast.

**TEKS 4.4H** is dividing up to a four-digit dividend by a one-digit divisor and interpreting remainders in word problems. This is the one that quietly costs points. Kids can do the division and still pick the wrong answer because they don't ask what the remainder means. If 30 guests sit at tables of 4, you need 8 tables, not 7 — the two leftover guests still need a seat. A Thanksgiving dinner table is the perfect place to practice that decision.

**TEKS 4.8C** is solving measurement problems — length, intervals of time, liquid volume, mass, and money — using whichever operation fits. It's a readiness standard, so it shows up on STAAR a lot. Turkey cook times, cups of gravy, pounds of potatoes, and the grocery bill at the harvest market all live here, and many of them are multi-step.

## Where Thanksgiving math usually goes sideways

**Bare facts with a seasonal border.** A page of 3/6 + 2/6 problems with a turkey in the corner isn't STAAR practice. STAAR 2.0 items are word problems, and kids need reps reading the story, picking the operation, and checking whether the answer makes sense.

**Remainders treated as an afterthought.** If students only ever write "R2," they never practice the real question: round up, drop it, or use it as the answer? Build at least a few problems where each of those is the right call.

**Measurement that stops after step one.** Many 4.8C problems take two steps — find the total, then the change, or convert and then compare. Kids who stop after the first calculation pick a distractor that's sitting right there in the answer choices.

**A whole-week "unit" nobody has time for.** Thanksgiving week might only be two or three days. You need something that runs in one class period and still gives you data to look at afterward.

## What a useful Grade 4 Thanksgiving escape room does

1. **Uses word problems, not bare computation.** Every item tells a short feast story so kids practice reading the way STAAR asks them to.
2. **Names the TEKS on the student page.** 4.3E, 4.4H, and 4.8C, labeled where kids and teachers can see them.
3. **Self-checks.** Google Slides in Present mode loop a wrong answer back with a hint, so you're not the only answer key in a room of 22 excited kids.
4. **Has a paper trail.** A printable recording sheet lets you see who guessed through and who actually worked it.
5. **Fits one class period.** About 45 to 60 minutes, in pairs, with no calculator — the same way Grade 4 STAAR Math is taken.

## Closing

Thanksgiving week can be fun and still move STAAR readiness. Keep the feast story, use real word problems for fractions, remainders, and measurement, and pick an activity that fits in one class period and leaves you with something to look at afterward. Your fourth graders will still head home talking about pie — they'll just have done the math to earn it.
