TEA's [2026–2027 testing calendar](https://tea2.tea.texas.gov/data-reports/test-administration/student-assessment-testing-calendar-2026-2027-updated.pdf) puts English I in the STAAR EOC window of November 30 through December 11, 2026. December 11 is the last day for make-up tests. Districts choose the day inside that window. From November 2, that leaves the weeks before Thanksgiving, then the window opens the Monday after the break.

Use one of those weeks for **TEKS E1.9C**: revising a draft so the writing is clearer, fuller, better organized, and said in words that fit the audience. The two standards that get tangled with it on the same test are **E1.9D** (editing for conventions) and **E1.8A** (author's purpose, audience, and message).

## What E1.9C actually asks

TEA's English I assessed curriculum, the list in effect for this year's EOC, puts E1.9C under the writing process. Paraphrased from that document, students revise drafts to improve clarity, development, organization, style, diction, and sentence effectiveness. That includes parallel constructions and where phrases and dependent clauses sit. TEA marks it a readiness standard ([TEA, STAAR English I Assessment, Eligible TEKS, beginning April 2023](https://tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/2023-english-i-assessed-curriculum-rla.pdf)).

In a freshman class that sounds like six questions a student can actually ask:

1. **Clarity.** Can a reader tell what this sentence is doing?
2. **Development.** If I add a sentence, does it build *this* paragraph's idea?
3. **Organization.** Is this sentence in the paragraph where it belongs?
4. **Style and diction.** Is this the word I'd use for this audience, or a fancier word that says less?
5. **Sentence effectiveness.** Do the items in a list match, and is each phrase attached to the right noun?
6. **Placement.** If I move a dependent clause, does the sentence say something different?

Editing is the other job. **E1.9D** is editing drafts for standard English conventions. The umbrella standard is marked supporting. Inside it, **E1.9D(i)** is readiness: complete, controlled sentences, and staying away from accidental comma splices, run-ons, and fragments. The rest of E1.9D covers verb tense and voice, pronoun-antecedent agreement, capitalization, punctuation that sets off phrases and clauses, and spelling. Same TEA document.

**E1.8A** is the reading partner: analyze the author's purpose, audience, and message within a text. On the current assessed-curriculum list it is a readiness standard too.

Revising and editing items show up in fiction, informational text, literary nonfiction, argumentative text, correspondence, and persuasive text. A drill that only uses one tired paragraph about "my summer" doesn't look like the test.

## The misses that eat the item

**Treating every underlined sentence as an editing item.** A comma splice is E1.9D. Moving a sentence so the paragraph stays on one idea is E1.9C. Students who only hunt commas will "fix" a sentence that needed to move.

**Adding a sentence that is true and useless.** Development means the new sentence supports the controlling idea of that paragraph. A fact from a different unit, dropped in because it sounds smart, is the wrong revision.

**Swapping in a fancier word.** Diction gets better when the word is more exact for this audience. "Utilize" is not a revision of "use." "A locked storage room most students walk past" is a revision of "just a closet," because it tells the reader something.

**Calling a broken list a comma problem.** "To read the letter, looking at the photos, and to copy the names" needs the verbs to match: "to read the letter, look at the photos, and copy the names." The comma was never the work.

**Leaving a phrase on the wrong noun.** "Filed in a plastic bin, our club found the 1978 letter" says the club was filed in the bin. Move the phrase: "Our club found the 1978 letter filed in a plastic bin." That is sentence effectiveness, E1.9C, even though a comma is sitting there.

**Answering E1.8A with the topic.** "The passage is about a letter" names the topic. Purpose is what the author is trying to do. Audience is who it's for. Message is what that reader is supposed to understand when they finish.

## A 45-minute block

This version is the teach. You can run it with a projector and a half sheet. The draft below is a classroom original, not a released item.

**0:00–0:05, sort the job.** Project this sentence:

> The archive club meets every Thursday they look at old yearbooks.

Ask pairs to label two possible fixes before anyone rewrites the whole thing.

- Fix A: "The archive club meets every Thursday. They look at old yearbooks."
- Fix B: "The archive club meets every Thursday to page through old yearbooks and write down names the captions skipped."

Fix A is editing, E1.9D(i): it repairs a run-on. Fix B is revising, E1.9C: the second half now says what the club does and why it matters. Take one pair's answer out loud. You want the class to hear the difference before the draft work starts.

**0:05–0:20, one draft, three moves.** Controlling idea, written on the board: *The archive keeps student writing the yearbook left out.*

> The school archive is just a closet. Old newspapers sit in plastic bins. Our club opened a 1978 paper last week. A student had written a letter about the cafeteria. The letter was angry. Yearbooks are also in the closet. The dance photos are funny. People should visit. Filed in a plastic bin, our club found the 1978 letter.

Walk three moves and stop. Do not fix every sentence yourself.

1. **Organization.** "Yearbooks are also in the closet. The dance photos are funny." Those two sentences start a different paragraph. Have a student say where they would put them, or whether they belong in this draft at all.
2. **Development.** After the cafeteria letter, try an added sentence: "Her name never made the headline, but the letter was still filed." Then show the trap: "The cafeteria serves pizza on Fridays." It might be true. It does not develop the controlling idea.
3. **Placement and a list.** Fix "Filed in a plastic bin, our club found the 1978 letter" by moving the phrase onto the letter. If you have a minute left, put this list under the draft and have them make the verbs match: "to read the letter, looking at the photos, and to copy the names."

Diction can be a thirty-second aside on "just a closet." Ask what a ninth grader who has never been in the room needs to picture.

**0:20–0:25, check for understanding.** Project this item only. Students write the letter and a reason of about six words. No talking.

The controlling idea is still *the archive keeps student writing the yearbook left out.* The audience is ninth graders who have never opened the archive. Which revision does that job?

> The 1978 letter still matters.

- A. The 1978 letter is really old and kind of interesting.
- B. The 1978 letter is a primary source that provides historical context.
- C. The 1978 letter shows a student saying what the yearbook skipped, which is why our club opens the bin.
- D. The 1978 letter, which was written by a student, is a letter from 1978.

**C.** It develops the controlling idea in words a ninth grader can use. A is vague. B reaches for jargon and never says what the letter does. D repeats itself. If you want a fast read of the room, thumbs on C versus anything else, then one student reads a reason.

**0:25–0:43, they revise.** Pairs get the same draft on paper. The task is three marks, numbered: one sentence moved or cut for organization, one sentence added for development, one phrase moved so it attaches to the right noun. You circulate and look for the pizza-sentence problem (true, off idea) and the dangling phrase they "fixed" by adding a comma and leaving in the wrong place.

**0:43–0:45, exit.** Each pair stars the mark they argued about and writes E1.9C or E1.9D next to it. That star is tomorrow's opener. If a pair labeled a moved sentence as editing, you already know what to reteach.

A one-minute E1.8A add-on, if the exit is quiet: three blanks under the draft. Purpose, audience, message. "Inform ninth graders" is a purpose. "Ninth graders who walk past the closet" is an audience. "Student writing can matter even when the yearbook left it out" is a message. "Closets" is a topic. Collect it only if you have the minute. Don't let it eat the revising.

The codes on the listing are the work of this block: E1.9C, E1.9D, and E1.8A. Extended constructed response sits on other standards, so plan that composition on its own day.

## The morning after

Pull the recording sheets, or the starred marks from the paper draft. Put one item on the board. Ask pairs to name the standard before they name the answer: revising, editing, or purpose. Then the letter. The sentence you want to hear is something like, "We moved it because it was a different idea, so that's E1.9C," or "We fixed the splice, so that's E1.9D." That sort, done in five minutes, is the skill the December window is actually checking.

The calendar will not move for a pizza party or a pep rally. One clean revising block this week, and a room that makes students tell revising from editing on fresh passages, is enough to walk into Thanksgiving with the standard still warm.
