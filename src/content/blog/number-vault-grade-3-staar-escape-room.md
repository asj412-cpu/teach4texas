One search keeps showing up in our store's TPT search data: **3.4K math TEKS**. Its neighbors are **math TEKS 3.4A** and **3rd grade TEKS math**. Nobody types that while browsing for something cute. It's a third-grade teacher with a standard number on the unit calendar who needs practice that matches it.

Most of what turns up is a fact-fluency page: 7 × 4 = ?, thirty times over. Facts matter, but a fluent kid can still miss the 3.4K item. The test doesn't stop at one fact. It tells a short story with two steps and checks whether the student finishes both of them.

Here's the fix in classroom terms, and one game that practices it.

## What 3.4K actually asks

Paraphrased, TEKS 3.4K asks students to **solve one-step and two-step multiplication and division problems within 100**. They can use objects, pictures (arrays, area models, equal groups), properties of operations, or facts they know. TEA labels it a **readiness standard** for Grade 3 math ([TEA, Grade 3 Mathematics Assessment, Eligible TEKS, revised August 2026](https://tea2.tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/sst-3-math-assessed-curriculum.pdf)). The same document says these content standards stay the same for the Student Success Tool, which TEA plans to start in fall 2027.

The key word is **two-step**. In a 3.4K story, the multiplication or division is usually only the first half. Then something gets added, taken away, or handed out, and that second step is where the points are lost.

STAAR doesn't allow a calculator on the Grades 3–7 math tests unless a student is eligible for one as a designated support ([TEA STAAR Calculator Policy](https://tea2.tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/staar-calculator-policy.pdf)). Practice should run pencil-only too.

## The three misses that cost points

**1. Stopping after step one.**
"Ms. Garza has 7 packs of pencils with 4 pencils in each pack. She hands out 9 pencils. How many are left?" The student finds 7 × 4 = 28, sees 28 in the answer choices, and bubbles it. On a well-built item, the step-one answer is always one of the wrong choices. The fix is a habit: **reread the last sentence before choosing.** "How many are *left*" means the story isn't over at 28. The answer is 28 − 9 = 19.

**2. Adding the two numbers that should be multiplied.**
"3 tables, 7 students at each table." Kids who grab the numbers and add get 10. Ask out loud: *Is 7 one table or every table?* Seven is the size of each group, so there are 3 groups of 7, and 3 × 7 = 21. Drawing three circles with 7 dots each settles it in ten seconds.

**3. Treating division as "take away."**
"63 crayons go into boxes of 9. How many boxes?" A student who subtracts once gets 54 and stops. Teach the question behind division: *How many groups of 9 fit in 63?* Then check it with the fact family: 7 × 9 = 63, so 7 boxes. If the story adds "then 4 more crayons go in the last box," the two-step version is 63 ÷ 9 = 7 boxes, and the last box now holds 9 + 4 = 13 crayons.

## Why 3.4K needs 3.5B right next to it

On TEA's Grade 3 list, 3.4K sits in the same strand, *Computations and Algebraic Relationships*, as two other readiness standards:

- **3.4A**: one-step and two-step addition and subtraction within 1,000 (the second step of many 3.4K stories),
- **3.5B**: represent and solve one-step and two-step multiplication and division problems within 100 using arrays, strip diagrams and equations.

3.5B is the cure for miss #1. When a student has to *show* the problem, step two can't vanish. For the pencil story above:

- **Strip diagram:** a bar split into 7 equal parts labeled 4, for 28 in all. The 9 pencils handed out are marked off one end, and the leftover piece is labeled *p*.
- **Equation:** p = (7 × 4) − 9.

Now a student who picks 28 can see that 28 is the whole bar, not the piece the question asks about. The matching STAAR skill runs the other direction too: read a story, then pick the diagram or equation that fits. The traps there are a bar that adds 7 + 4 instead of showing 7 equal groups, and an equation that subtracts before multiplying.

## What a good Grade 3 review block looks like

- **Two-step stories, not bare facts.** Most items should make students do something after the multiplication.
- **Wrong answers that show real mistakes.** The step-one answer, the sum of the two factors, and the "added instead of subtracted" total should all be in the choices. Then a miss tells you something.
- **A hint, then another try.** A wrong tap should bring up a strategy ("What happens after the equal groups?"), not the answer.
- **Models on the menu.** At least a few items should ask for the strip diagram or the equation, not just the number.
- **Pencil work on paper.** A recording sheet keeps the steps visible so you can see *where* a pair stopped.

## After the game: the debrief that sticks

Collect the recording sheets and find the most-missed item. In a 3.4K unit it's almost always a Two-Step Protocol item where pairs chose the step-one answer. Put that one story on the board the next morning. Have pairs draw the strip diagram and point to the piece the question asks for. Two minutes of that does more than another fact page.

## The short version

Teachers who search "3.4K math TEKS" usually have fact practice that doesn't match the test. Teach the finish, not just the fact: *What happens after the equal groups? Which piece of the bar is the question about?* Then give students a round where finishing both steps unlocks something.
