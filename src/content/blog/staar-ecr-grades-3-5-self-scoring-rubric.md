Ask a fourth grader how their STAAR essay went and you'll usually hear, "Good, I wrote a lot." Ask how it would score and you'll get a shrug. That shrug is the problem. Most Grades 3–5 students have never looked at their own essay the way a scorer does, so they can't tell a thin essay from a complete one.

This is the season Texas RLA teachers start building extended constructed response (ECR) practice into the week. The essays you'll collect between now and spring get much better when students know the two questions the rubric asks. Here's what those questions are, the misses that keep kids at a 2 out of 5, and a routine that gets students scoring their own drafts.

## What the ECR actually is

Every STAAR RLA test in Grades 3–5 includes an essay. Students read a passage (or a pair of passages) and write in one of two modes, informational or argumentative, using evidence from the text ([TEA, STAAR Spring 2026 Grade 4 RLA Scoring Guide](https://tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/released-test-questions/2026-staar-4-rla-scoring-guide.pdf)). TEA posts the rubrics and released scoring guides on its [STAAR Reading Language Arts Resources](https://tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/staar-reading-language-arts-resources) page.

TEA scores the essay with a 5-point rubric that has two separate traits ([TEA, Scoring Process for STAAR Constructed Responses](https://tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/scoring-process-staar-constructed-response-1.pdf)):

- **Development and organization of ideas, 0–3.** Is there a clear central idea (or opinion)? Does the essay hold together from beginning to end? Is the text evidence relevant, and is it explained? Are the ideas expressed clearly?
- **Conventions, 0–2.** How well does the student control grade-level sentences, punctuation, capitalization, grammar and spelling? The key question is whether the errors get in the way of the meaning.

Three details from TEA's documents matter for how you teach:

1. **A 0 for ideas means a 0 for conventions.** TEA's Grades 3–5 rubrics say so directly. Clean spelling can't rescue an essay with nothing to score ([Informational rubric, Grades 3–5](https://tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/tx-staar-informational-rubric-g3-5.pdf); [Argument/opinion rubric, Grades 3–5](https://tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/tx-staar-arg-opinion-rubric-g3-5.pdf)).
2. **Two scorers, so the real scale is 0–10.** Each essay is scored by two scorers, and the two scores are added together (same Grade 4 scoring guide). In class, one score out of 5 is plenty.
3. **With paired passages, one text is enough.** At Grades 3–5, the rubric accepts evidence drawn from at least one of the texts. Kids who think they have to quote both passages often run out of time.

## The TEKS behind the essay

TEA's Grade 4 RLA assessed curriculum, revised August 2026, lists the ECR standards as 4.7(B), writing responses that show understanding of texts, and 4.12(B) and 4.12(C), composing informational and argumentative/opinion texts. The Grade 3 and Grade 5 documents list the same three codes (3.7(B), 3.12(B), 3.12(C) and 5.7(B), 5.12(B), 5.12(C)). The August revision removed readiness labels from these writing standards, but TEA says every standard affected by the revisions will still be assessed. The changes take effect with the Student Success Tool in fall 2027 ([TEA, Grade 4 RLA Eligible TEKS, revised August 2026](https://tea2.tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/sst-4-rla-assessed-curriculum.pdf)).

The standard doing the most work is **3.7(C), 4.7(C) and 5.7(C): use text evidence to support an appropriate response.** It is a readiness standard at all three grades. That's the evidence-and-explanation skill the ideas trait rewards.

## A practice passage to try

Here's a short original passage and prompt you can use tomorrow. It isn't a released STAAR item.

> **The Corner Lot**
>
> For three years, the empty lot at Pecan and Third held nothing but weeds and a broken shopping cart. Then Ms. Ortega's third graders asked the city if they could plant a garden there. The city said yes, and a hardware store donated six shovels. The class planted milkweed, lantana and two rows of peppers, and they watered every Tuesday and Friday after school. By May, monarch butterflies were stopping on the milkweed. Mr. Haskins, who walks his dog past the lot every morning, started bringing a folding chair so he could sit and watch them. In June, the class set a basket of peppers by the sidewalk with a sign: *Take some. They're free.* The basket was empty by lunch.

**Prompt:** Write an informational essay explaining how the garden changed the corner lot for the neighborhood. Use evidence from the passage.

## The four misses that cap scores

**1. Retelling instead of a central idea.**
A first draft often reads: "First the lot had weeds. Then the class asked the city. Then they planted milkweed…" That's a summary, and it never says what the garden *did*. Have students finish one sentence before they write anything else: "The garden changed the lot from ___ to ___." For example: *The garden turned a lot nobody used into a place where neighbors stopped, sat and shared.* Every paragraph after that has to prove it.

**2. Evidence with no explanation.**
"Mr. Haskins brought a folding chair." Then the paragraph ends. The quote is relevant, but the reader has to guess why it matters. Teach the follow-up sentence: *"This shows…"* or *"That matters because…"* Here: *A neighbor who used to walk right past now plans to stay, so the lot became a place people want to be.* Evidence plus explanation is the difference between a thin essay and a complete one.

**3. One detail, used twice.**
Students find one good detail and repeat it in every paragraph. A complete essay needs two or three *different* moments that each support the central idea. From "The Corner Lot": the monarchs (the lot is alive now), Mr. Haskins's chair (people stay), and the empty pepper basket (neighbors share). Three details, three paragraphs, one idea.

**4. Thinking conventions can carry the essay.**
Some careful spellers write four tidy sentences and expect a good score. Conventions are worth only 2 of the 5 points, and they drop to 0 when the ideas score is 0. Go the other direction, too. A strong essay with a few errors can still earn a 2 in conventions, because the question is whether the errors get in the way of the reader.

## The routine: "score it before I do"

TEA's rubric is written for trained scorers. Third graders need it in their own words, and they need it where they can see it. Here's a routine that takes about ten minutes after a timed practice essay:

1. **Students pick a score for each trait.** For ideas, they choose 3, 2, 1 or 0. For conventions, they choose 2, 1 or 0. Each choice comes with one sentence of proof: *"I gave myself a 2 because I used the monarchs but didn't explain them."*
2. **You score the same essay.** Use TEA's rubric and the anchor papers in the released scoring guides to keep your own scoring honest.
3. **Compare and pick one next step.** The gap between the student's score and yours is the lesson. Write one next step on the paper, like "add a second moment" or "explain the chair."
4. **Track by student number.** Record each total out of 5 by practice round so you can see whether the class is moving from 2s to 3s over the winter.

The goal is for students, after a few rounds, to point to the wall and say which score their draft matches before you sit down.

## The short version

The ECR asks two questions: *Is the idea clear, supported and explained?* and *Do the errors get in the way?* Students who can answer both about their own draft write better drafts. Teach the central-idea sentence, the "this shows" follow-up and the three-different-moments plan. Then put the rubric on the wall in words a nine-year-old can use, and have students score their essays before you do.
