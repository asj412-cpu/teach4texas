If your Grade 3–5 kids can circle nouns on a Monday drill and still freeze on a passage-based editing set on Thursday, you already know the gap. STAAR RLA does not ask them to label parts of speech in isolation. It asks them to **fix the draft** — combine sentences, catch conventions, straighten subject-verb agreement, and choose the revision that keeps the author’s meaning.

## The TEKS that show up when editing gets real

Talk about them the way you plan, not as a standards dump.

**TEKS 3.11D** is editing drafts using standard English conventions of grammar, spelling, capitalization, punctuation, and sentences. In the room that means: can they *see* the error in context and fix it without you tapping the line?

**TEKS 4.11D** raises the bar — subject-verb agreement, pronouns, commas in compound sentences, and spelling patterns that trip kids who “sound it out” too confidently. The passage still has to make sense after the edit.

**TEKS 5.11D** pushes into more complex sentence craft and verb tense consistency across a longer edit set. The distractors get meaner. One “sounds fine” choice quietly breaks the author’s meaning.

Those three travel together in a Grades 3–5 escape format because stations can escalate: clarity first, then conventions, then grammar, then spelling/homophones, then a mixed STAAR-style passage. One skill sheet cannot carry that arc.

## Classroom pitfalls that look like “they know grammar”

**Parts-of-speech worksheets that never become edits.** Kids can underline verbs all morning and still pick the wrong combined sentence because they never practiced *revising for clarity* under a little time pressure.

**Escape rooms that are only vibes.** A lock icon and a Google Form do not equal STAAR editing. If the questions are bare grammar trivia, kids remember the theme and forget the passage. You want missions that mirror editing/revising item energy — fix the draft, keep the meaning, earn the badge.

**One giant packet with no recovery path.** When every miss dumps the group into silence, you become the answer key. Self-checking tips matter. Students should be able to recover, re-read the sentence, and try again while you coach the sticky misconceptions (pronoun reference, comma splices, homophones that sound right).

**Skipping the mixed passage.** Isolated sentences are warm-ups. STAAR finishes with a passage-based edit. If your practice never gets there, Thursday’s CFA will feel like a different sport.

## What a good grammar escape block looks like

Keep setup light enough for a Tuesday literacy block or a Friday stations day.

1. **Present mode or assigned slides.** One mission at a time. No scavenger chaos required.
2. **Pairs with a talk job.** “Read the sentence out loud. What changes if we pick B?”
3. **Rank badges or chips that build an escape code.** Correct edits earn progress. Wrong edits route to a hint, not a shame spiral.
4. **Self-checking tips.** Sergeant-style coaching beats you narrating every distractor.
5. **A mission tracker on paper.** Even when the game is digital, pencil work keeps kids honest about what they changed and why.

Five missions that escalate, then a short code collection at the end, is a sweet spot for one class period or a two-day station rotation.
