Read this sentence out loud to a fourth grader: *"The stack of library books on my desk are due Friday."* Most of them will nod. It sounds fine, because the word right before the verb is *desk*, and a few words back is *books*. Their ears grab the closest noun and match the verb to it.

That habit is the whole problem with subject-verb agreement in Grade 4. Kids don't miss it because they've never heard the rule. They miss it because the sentence hides the real subject behind a few extra words, and they edit by sound instead of by structure. STAAR revising and editing questions are full of sentences like that. Here's what the standard asks, the four traps behind most misses, and a routine that gets kids hunting for the true subject on their own.

## What the TEKS actually ask

TEA's Grade 4 RLA assessed curriculum puts this skill in Reporting Strand 2, Writing, under **revising and editing**. Students revise and edit texts in several genres, including fiction, informational, argumentative and persuasive writing, and even correspondence like letters ([TEA, Grade 4 RLA Eligible TEKS, revised August 2026](https://tea2.tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/sst-4-rla-assessed-curriculum.pdf)).

The editing standard is **4.11(D): edit drafts using standard English conventions**. Three of its parts carry most of the grammar you'll teach this winter:

- **4.11(D)(i):** complete simple and compound sentences with subject-verb agreement, without splices, run-ons or fragments. In the August 2026 revision, this is still a **readiness standard**.
- **4.11(D)(vii):** pronouns, including reflexive pronouns like *herself* and *themselves*. This is a supporting standard.
- **4.11(D)(x):** punctuation, including apostrophes in possessives, commas in compound sentences and quotation marks in dialogue. This is also a supporting standard.

The August revision changed one editing label: 4.11(D)(xi), spelling, moved from readiness to supporting. TEA says every standard affected by the revisions will still be assessed, and the changes take effect when the Student Success Tool launches in fall 2027 (same TEA document). So agreement is the piece of the editing standard that stays on the readiness list. It's worth real practice time.

## The four traps behind most agreement misses

**1. The noun in the middle.**
*"The stack of library books on my desk ___ due Friday."* The subject is *stack*, one stack, so the verb is *is*. Kids pick *are* because *books* is plural and close by. Any phrase starting with *of*, *in*, *on* or *with* can sit between the subject and the verb and pull the ear the wrong way.

**2. Words that sound plural but count as one.**
*Each*, *everyone*, *everybody* and *one of* all take a singular verb. *"Each of the Bluebonnet nominees ___ a sticker on the cover"* takes *has*, not *have*. Group nouns work the same way in Grade 4 practice: *the choir*, *the team* and *the class* are one group, so *"The choir is ready for its concert."* Watch for a second miss hiding in that sentence: kids who write *are* usually write *their* too.

**3. Two subjects joined by *and*.**
*"Abuela and my cousin ___ pan dulce on Sundays."* Two people means a plural subject, so the verb is *bake*. Kids often match the verb to the noun closest to it, *cousin*, and write *bakes*.

**4. *Me and…* at the front of the sentence.**
*"Me and Daniela was late to lunch"* has two problems. The pronoun is wrong (it should be *Daniela and I*), and the subject is plural, so the verb is *were*. Pronoun and agreement errors travel together, which is why it helps to teach them side by side.

## The routine: cover the middle

Kids need a move they can do with a pencil, not just a rule they can recite. This one takes about two minutes to teach and works on almost every agreement question.

1. **Cover the middle.** Put a finger, or a light pencil line, over any phrase between the subject and the verb: *of library books*, *on my desk*, *in the window*.
2. **Ask "who or what is doing it?"** Read what's left: *"The stack … are due Friday."* Now the error is easy to hear.
3. **Swap in *it* or *they*.** If the subject could be replaced with *it*, use the singular verb (*it is*, *it has*). If it could be replaced with *they*, use the plural (*they are*, *they have*). Two subjects joined by *and* always swap to *they*.
4. **Check the pronoun that points back.** If the subject is *it*, any pronoun that refers back to it is *its*, not *their*.

For commas in compound sentences, use a quick partner test. Cover the joining word (*and*, *but*, *or*, *so*). If both halves could stand alone as sentences, the comma goes **before** the joining word: *"The bus was late, so we missed the assembly."* If the second half can't stand alone, as in *"We waited and waited for the bus,"* there's no compound sentence and no comma before *and*.

## A practice letter to try

STAAR editing happens inside a text, not in a list of single sentences, so practice that way too. Here's a short original letter with three editing questions. It isn't a released STAAR item.

> **A Letter to Coach Ramirez**
>
> (1) Dear Coach Ramirez,
>
> (2) Our class wants to thank you for Field Day. (3) The box of ribbons on the gym table were a great surprise. (4) Everyone in our class have a ribbon on the classroom wall now. (5) Lucas and Priya says the water balloon race was the best part. (6) The sack race was hard but we still had fun.
>
> (7) Sincerely,
>
> (8) Ms. Tran's fourth graders

**Question 1.** What is the correct way to write sentence 3?
- A) The box of ribbons on the gym table are a great surprise.
- B) The box of ribbons on the gym table was a great surprise.
- C) The boxes of ribbons on the gym table was a great surprise.
- D) No change

**Question 2.** What change should be made in sentence 4?
- A) Change *have* to *has*
- B) Change *our* to *their*
- C) Change *wall* to *walls*
- D) No change

**Question 3.** What is the correct way to write sentence 6?
- A) The sack race was hard, but we still had fun.
- B) The sack race was hard but, we still had fun.
- C) The sack race, was hard but we still had fun.
- D) No change

**Answers:** 1-B (cover *of ribbons on the gym table*, and the subject is *box*), 2-A (*everyone* is singular), 3-A (two complete sentences joined by *but*, with the comma before it). Did you notice sentence 5? *Lucas and Priya* is a compound subject, so it should be *say*. Ask students to find it on their own. It's the one that shows whether they're really checking subjects or just answering the questions in front of them.

## The short version

Fourth graders don't need another chart that says "singular subject, singular verb." They need a habit: cover the middle, find who or what is doing it, swap in *it* or *they*, then check the pronoun that points back. Practice it in sentences, then in a short letter or passage, until students catch the error you didn't ask about.
