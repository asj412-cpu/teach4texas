One search in our store's TPT search data reads like a unit plan: **cell cycle, cancer & differentiation escape room**. That's a Biology teacher who already knows the three ideas belong together and wants one review block that treats them that way.

Most cell-cycle review doesn't do that. It's a stack of "name this phase" pictures, then a separate cancer worksheet, then differentiation squeezed in as a vocabulary word. Students can label prophase and still not explain why a root grows, why a broken checkpoint matters, or how a nerve cell and a muscle cell can carry the same DNA.

Here's how the three standards connect, the misconceptions worth targeting, and one game we built to practice them.

## What B.6 actually asks

TEA's Biology eligible-TEKS document puts these under one knowledge statement: the student knows how an organism grows and the importance of cell differentiation ([TEA, STAAR Biology Assessment, Eligible TEKS, effective 2025–2026](https://tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/2026-staar-bio-assessed-curriculum.pdf)). Paraphrased:

- **B.6A (supporting):** explain why the cell cycle matters for growth, including an overview of its stages and the models of DNA replication.
- **B.6B (supporting):** explain how cells specialize through differentiation, including the role of environmental factors.
- **B.6C (readiness):** relate disruptions of the cell cycle to diseases such as cancer.

That readiness label is worth noticing. The cancer standard is the one TEA marks as readiness, and it only makes sense if students understand the normal cycle first. So B.6A is the foundation, B.6C is where the points are, and B.6B is the "same DNA, different job" idea that keeps students from thinking every change in a cell is cancer.

## The misconceptions that cost points

**1. "The cell cycle is mitosis."**
Most of a cell's life is interphase, and that's where it grows and copies its DNA. A quick data check makes this concrete. Say a lab group counts 250 cells in a root-tip squash and finds 205 in interphase and 45 dividing. That's 82% in interphase. The share of cells in a stage reflects the share of time the cycle spends there, so interphase is the long part and mitosis is the short burst at the end.

**2. Losing track of the DNA.**
Ask students to track DNA amount with plain numbers. If a body cell starts G1 with 4 units, it has 8 after S phase. Mitosis and cytokinesis then split that into two cells with 4 each. If students can tell this story, they can read a DNA-per-cell graph without memorizing what it looks like.

**3. Mixing up mitosis and meiosis.**
Growth and repair run on mitosis. A human body cell with 46 chromosomes divides into two cells with 46 each, identical to the parent. Four cells with 23 is meiosis, which makes gametes. Many wrong answers on this topic are meiosis facts placed in a growth question, so train students to ask, "Is this growth, or is this making sex cells?"

**4. Treating DNA replication models as trivia.**
"Semi-conservative" means each new DNA molecule keeps one original strand and builds one new strand. Have students draw it with two colors. Start with one molecule and replicate three times. They get 8 molecules, and only 2 of them still carry an original strand. That's ¼, and students who drew it can explain why the number isn't 0 or ½.

**5. "Cancer means cells grow too fast."**
The better explanation is control. The cycle has checkpoints that act like brakes, plus growth signals that act like a gas pedal. Cancer develops when mutations break the brakes or jam the gas pedal so a cell keeps dividing when it should stop. The result can be a tumor, and cells that invade other tissues can spread. Try this one with your class: *"A mutation leaves a growth-signal protein stuck 'on' even with no signal from outside the cell. What happens to the cycle, and why could that lead to cancer?"* A strong answer names the disruption and then the result.

**6. Thinking specialized cells have different DNA.**
Nearly every body cell in an organism carries the same DNA. Differentiation is about which genes are turned on. Environmental factors such as chemical signals from nearby cells help steer that choice, which is why stem cells grown in a lab can be pushed toward different cell types by changing the signals in the dish. Once students see differentiation as "same instructions, different pages read," they stop confusing it with mutation or cancer.

## What a good B.6 review block looks like

- **Data, not just diagrams.** Cell counts, DNA amounts over time, and replication results make students reason instead of recall.
- **Wrong answers that test the real confusions.** The best wrong choices are meiosis facts in a growth question, "cancer is just fast growth," and "differentiated cells have different DNA."
- **Cancer last, built on the normal cycle.** B.6C questions land better after students can explain what the checkpoint normally does.
- **A hint, then another try.** A miss should show a strategy, not the answer.
- **A mixed final round.** Stages, replication and cancer in one set, closer to how a real test mixes them.

Collect the recording sheets and find the most-missed question. In a B.6 unit it's often a cancer question that a team answered with "the cells grow faster," or a growth question where a meiosis answer looked right. Put that one on the board the next morning and ask pairs to explain the disruption and its result in one sentence each. That's exactly the reasoning B.6C asks for.
