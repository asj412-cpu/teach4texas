Early September in a Texas Grade 3 room has a familiar soundtrack. You can hear the fact chants. *Six times seven is forty-two.* Someone still whispers skip-counts on their fingers. Then you put an array in front of them — or a strip diagram with one part missing — and the room goes quiet.

That stall is not laziness. It is the gap between **saying** a fact and **using** multiplication as a structure. STAAR Category 2 (Computations and Algebraic Relationships) leans hard on that second skill. ## The three TEKS that show up when multiplication gets real

Name them the way you talk about them in planning, not as a standards dump.

**TEKS 3.4E** is automaticity with multiplication facts through 10 × 10, plus the related division facts. Automaticity here means students can retrieve and use the fact without spending the whole item counting by ones. Related division matters because STAAR loves to flip the same relationship: if 8 × 6 = 48, then 48 ÷ 6 = 8 has to feel like the same family, not a brand-new trick.

**TEKS 3.4H** is arrays up to 10 × 10. Arrays are not decoration. They are the bridge from equal groups to rows-and-columns thinking. Students should be able to build, read, and describe an array — and connect it to both a multiplication equation and a related division equation.

**TEKS 3.5B** is representing and solving one- and two-step multiplication and division problems within 100 using pictorial models (including strip diagrams) and equations. This is where Category 2 gets spicy. The story is longer. The model has to match. The equation has to match the model. One wrong part label, and the whole answer drifts.

Those three standards travel together in September. Fact fluency without arrays stays fragile. Arrays without equations stay pretty. Equations without strip diagrams stay abstract. A good practice block hits all three in the same sitting.

## Classroom pitfalls that look like “they know their facts”

**Facts-only fluency that never transfers.** Timed sheets can help retrieval, but they do not teach kids to *see* multiplication in a problem. A student can blaze through 7 × 8 flash cards and still circle the wrong equation for a stamp story because they never practiced choosing a representation under a little pressure.

**Arrays as decoration.** You know this one: the worksheet shows a tidy array, and kids ignore it and start skip-counting from the numbers in the stem. Or they count every dot as if place value just left the building. The array has to *do work* — identify rows, columns, total, and the matching equation.

**Strip diagrams without equation match.** Students draw a bar, drop numbers into boxes that feel “about right,” and never check whether the equation says the same thing. Whole and parts get swapped. A two-step story collapses into one operation. On STAAR 2.0-style items, that mismatch is exactly what distractors are built for.

**Mystery games that are only vibes.** A lock icon and a Google Form do not equal TEKS practice. If the questions are bare fact drills, or the “code” is unrelated to the math, kids remember the theme and forget the model. You want chips, clues, and a final code that students earn by doing the Category 2 work — not by guessing.

## What a good mystery practice block looks like

Keep the setup light enough for a Tuesday math block.

1. **Present mode on the board (or shared screen).** One scene at a time. No scavenger chaos if your room is not built for it.
2. **Pairs or trios with a clear talk job.** “Point to the array / strip / equation that matches. Defend it in one sentence.”
3. **Answer chips that build a code.** Correct choices unlock colored chips or clue pieces. Wrong choices do not shame — they route to a hint that pushes kids back to the model.
4. **Self-checking with hints.** Students should be able to recover without you narrating every distractor. Your job becomes circulating for the sticky misconceptions: part/whole swaps, array row/column confusion, related-division blind spots.
5. **A printable recording sheet.** Even when the game is digital, pencil work keeps the strip diagram honest. Kids write the equation that matches the picture before they claim the chip.

Aim for a complete run that feels like a mission, not a worksheet cosplay. Four scenes that escalate, then a short Final Code boss that pulls the threads together, is a sweet spot for a single class period or a two-day station.
