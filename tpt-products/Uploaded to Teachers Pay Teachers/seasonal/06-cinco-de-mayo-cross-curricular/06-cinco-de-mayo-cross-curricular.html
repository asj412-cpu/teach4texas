Reading Passage 1: What is Cinco de Mayo Really About?

Passage (Lexile 780-840)

If you ask most people in the United States what Cinco de Mayo is, they might say “Mexican Independence Day.” But that’s actually not correct! Cinco de Mayo celebrates something very different — and the real story is even more exciting.

Cinco de Mayo, which means “the fifth of May” in Spanish, celebrates the Battle of Puebla on May 5, 1862. On that day, the Mexican army defeated the French army in the city of Puebla, Mexico. What makes this so remarkable is that the French were considered the most powerful military in the world at the time. The Mexican army was much smaller, less equipped, and not expected to win. But they did.

Mexico owed money to several European countries, including France. When Mexico couldn’t pay, France’s emperor, Napoleon III, decided to invade Mexico and take control of the country. He sent thousands of well-trained, well-armed French soldiers to Mexico.

General Ignacio Zaragoza led the Mexican forces at Puebla. Though his army was smaller and had fewer weapons, they used their knowledge of the local terrain — the hills, valleys, and muddy fields — to their advantage. Against all odds, they defeated the French that day.

It’s important to know that this was one battle, not the end of the war. France eventually took control of parts of Mexico for a few years before being pushed out. And Mexican Independence Day is actually September 16, not May 5.

So why do we celebrate Cinco de Mayo so enthusiastically in the United States? The holiday became popular among Mexican Americans as a celebration of Mexican culture, heritage, and the spirit of standing up against impossible odds. In Texas, Cinco de Mayo has been celebrated since the 1860s — making it one of the longest-running cultural celebrations in the state!

Today, Cinco de Mayo is a day to celebrate Mexican-American culture with music, dancing, food, and community. It’s a reminder that courage and determination can overcome even the greatest challenges.

Math Word Problems Set 1: Battle Math

  1. General Zaragoza had 4,500 Mexican soldiers. The French had 6,500 soldiers. How many MORE soldiers did the French have? (Answer: 2,000)

  2. If the Mexican army marched 18 miles per day for 5 days to reach Puebla, how many total miles did they march? (Answer: 90 miles)

  3. The battle lasted about 4 hours. If cannons fired every 15 minutes, how many times did the cannons fire during the battle? (Answer: 16 times)

  4. (Gr 4-5) After the battle, 462 French soldiers were captured. If they were put in groups of 6, how many full groups could be formed? How many soldiers would be left over? (Answer: 77 groups, 0 left over)

  5. (Gr 5) The French army brought 3,200 pounds of supplies. They used 2/5 of the supplies before the battle. How many pounds did they use? How many pounds were left? (Answer: Used 1,280 lbs; Left 1,920 lbs)

Reading Passage 2: The Tejano Contribution — Mexican-American Culture in Texas

Passage (Lexile 790-850)

Long before Texas was part of the United States — and even before it was the Republic of Texas — it was part of Mexico. The Mexican and Spanish settlers who lived in Texas before and during this time are known as Tejanos, and their contributions to Texas culture are everywhere, even if you don’t always realize it.

The word “Tejano” comes from the Spanish word for “Texan.” Tejano families have lived in Texas for hundreds of years. Cities like San Antonio, El Paso, and Laredo were founded by Spanish and Mexican settlers long before English-speaking settlers arrived. In fact, San Antonio was founded in 1718 — more than 100 years before Stephen F. Austin brought his colonists to Texas.

Tejano culture is woven into the fabric of Texas life. Many of the foods Texans love — tacos, tamales, enchiladas, salsa, and tortillas — come from Mexican and Tejano traditions. The ranching techniques that made Texas famous, including cattle branding and the use of lassos, were developed by Mexican vaqueros (cowboys). The American cowboy learned from the vaquero!

Tejano music is another beautiful contribution. It blends Mexican folk music with polka, rock, and other styles. Artists like Selena Quintanilla, who grew up in Corpus Christi, Texas, brought Tejano music to audiences around the world. Selena became one of the most beloved musicians in Texas history.

Architecture in Texas also shows Tejano influence. The Spanish missions, including the famous Alamo in San Antonio, were built in the Spanish colonial style. Many Texas homes, churches, and public buildings feature stucco walls, tile roofs, and courtyards that come from Spanish and Mexican building traditions.

Today, Mexican Americans make up nearly 40% of the Texas population, making them the largest ethnic group in the state. Tejano culture isn’t just part of Texas history — it IS Texas history.

Reading Passage 3: Mariachi — The Music of Celebration

Passage (Lexile 770-830)

If you’ve ever been to a Mexican restaurant, a quinceañera, or a Cinco de Mayo celebration, you’ve probably heard the sound of mariachi music. With its trumpets, violins, and guitars, mariachi is one of the most recognizable and joyful musical traditions in the world.

Mariachi music originated in western Mexico, in the state of Jalisco, during the 1800s. The word “mariachi” originally referred to the platform or stage where musicians performed. Over time, it came to mean the musicians themselves and their style of music.

A traditional mariachi group includes violins, trumpets, a Spanish guitar, a vihuela (a small round-backed guitar), and a guitarrón (a large bass guitar). The musicians wear elegant traje de charro — suits decorated with silver buttons and embroidery. These outfits are based on the clothing of Mexican horsemen called charros.

Mariachi songs tell stories about love, nature, patriotism, and everyday life. Some songs are happy and fast, perfect for dancing. Others are slow and emotional, expressing deep feelings. One of the most famous mariachi songs is “Cielito Lindo,” which includes the famous chorus “Ay, ay, ay, ay, canta y no llores” (“Sing and don’t cry”).

In Texas, mariachi is everywhere. Many schools have mariachi programs where students learn to play in the mariachi tradition. The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley has one of the most respected collegiate mariachi programs in the country. San Antonio hosts an annual Mariachi Festival that draws musicians from across the Americas.

In 2011, UNESCO (the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization) added mariachi to its list of Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. This means the entire world recognizes mariachi as an important cultural treasure worth protecting.

Whether it’s a wedding, a birthday, a holiday, or just a Tuesday evening, mariachi music has the power to bring people together and fill any space with joy.

Math Word Problems Set 3: Musical Math

  1. A mariachi group has 3 violins, 2 trumpets, 1 guitar, 1 vihuela, and 1 guitarrón. How many musicians are in the group? (Answer: 8)

  2. The group plays 4 songs per set and plays 3 sets at a party. Each song is about 5 minutes long. How many total minutes of music do they play? (Answer: 60 minutes)

  3. A mariachi suit costs $450. Silver buttons for decoration cost $12 each. If a suit has 35 buttons, what is the total cost of the suit with buttons? (Answer: $450 + $420 = $870)

  4. (Gr 4-5) At the San Antonio Mariachi Festival, 1,250 people attend on Friday, 2,380 on Saturday, and 1,875 on Sunday. What is the total attendance? What is the average daily attendance? (Answer: Total = 5,505; Average = 1,835)

Reading Passage 4: Frida Kahlo & Mexican Art in Texas

Passage (Lexile 780-840)

Walk through any Texas city and you’ll see it — vibrant murals on building walls, colorful art in galleries, and bold designs on everything from pottery to clothing. Much of this artistic spirit comes from Mexican and Mexican-American art traditions, and one of the most famous artists connected to this tradition is Frida Kahlo.

Frida Kahlo was born in 1907 in Coyoacán, Mexico. As a teenager, she was in a terrible bus accident that left her with lifelong injuries. During her long recovery, she began to paint. Art became her way of expressing her pain, her joy, and her identity.

Frida is best known for her self-portraits — paintings of herself. But these weren’t ordinary self-portraits. Frida filled her paintings with symbols from Mexican folk art: flowers, animals, bright colors, and references to Mexico’s indigenous (native) cultures. She proudly wore traditional Mexican clothing and incorporated pre-Columbian art styles into her work.

Frida’s art was revolutionary because she painted her real life — including her suffering, her Mexican heritage, and her identity as a woman — at a time when most famous artists were men painting landscapes and historical scenes. She showed the world that personal stories and cultural identity are worthy subjects for great art.

In Texas, Frida Kahlo’s influence is everywhere. Mexican-American muralists across the state create large, colorful public artworks that tell the stories of their communities. In San Antonio, Houston, Austin, and El Paso, you can find stunning murals celebrating Mexican-American history, culture, and identity. These murals are influenced by Frida and by other great Mexican muralists like Diego Rivera and David Alfaro Siqueiros.

The tradition of folk art — handmade pottery, textiles, papel picado (perforated paper banners), and piñatas — is also alive and well in Texas. At markets and festivals across the state, artisans create beautiful works that connect modern Texas to centuries-old Mexican artistic traditions.

Art has the power to tell stories, preserve culture, and bring communities together. The Mexican and Mexican-American art traditions in Texas do all three.

Math Word Problems Set 4: Art by Numbers

  1. An artist uses 3 cans of paint for a small mural and 8 cans for a large mural. If she paints 5 small murals and 2 large murals, how many cans of paint does she need? (Answer: 15 + 16 = 31 cans)

  2. A papel picado banner is 12 feet long. Each paper flag is 3/4 of a foot wide. How many flags are on the banner? (Answer: 16 flags)

  3. An art festival has 85 booths. Each booth pays $125 for their space. How much money does the festival collect from booths? (Answer: $10,625)

  4. (Gr 4-5) A muralist works 6.5 hours per day. She works on a mural for 8 days. How many total hours does she work? If she earns $22 per hour, how much does she earn? (Answer: 52 hours; $1,144)

  5. (Gr 5) A pottery artist makes 36 pieces. She sells 2/3 at a market for $18 each and donates the rest. How much money does she earn? (Answer: 24 pieces × $18 = $432)
