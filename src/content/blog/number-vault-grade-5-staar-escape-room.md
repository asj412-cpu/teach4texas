Teachers searching **5.4B equations**, **5.4B math escape room**, **math 5.3E**, and **TEKS 5.2 escape room** are not hunting a padlock sticker next to \(6 \times 7\). They need one review block where fifth graders practice three skills that keep showing up on Grade 5 STAAR Math — writing an equation with a letter for the unknown in a multi-step whole-number problem, multiplying decimals (money included) and putting the decimal point where it belongs, and reading a decimal through the thousandths in expanded notation.

If your “escape room” can be cleared by answering a page of bare facts behind a lock icon, you already know the gap. That is trivia cosplay. It is not STAAR practice.

The real ask: force-copy Google Slides, word problems instead of naked computation, calculator off (because Grade 5 STAAR Math is not a calculator test), and missions that make kids build the equation, multiply the decimals, and reread the question before they commit — not twenty flash cards with a countdown timer.

## The TEKS that matter in a Grade 5 review block

Talk about them the way you plan, not as a standards dump.

**TEKS 5.4B** is representing *and* solving multi-step problems with whole numbers, using an equation where a letter stands for the unknown. The word that matters is *represent*. A kid who can compute \(6 \times 24 - 58\) still freezes when the stem asks, “Which equation can be used to find \(m\), the number of markers left?” If the review never asks kids to choose or write \(m = (6 \times 24) - 58\) before they solve, it is not 5.4B practice.

**TEKS 5.3E** is solving for products of decimals to the hundredths, including money, using place-value strategies, properties of operations, and the connection to whole-number multiplication. In plain classroom language: \(2.4 \times 1.5\) is \(24 \times 15 = 360\), then place value tells you the product is \(3.60\). The STAAR trap is not the multiplication. It is the decimal point, and the question that wanted *all* the bottles instead of one crate.

**TEKS 5.2A** is representing the value of a digit in decimals through the thousandths, using expanded notation and numerals. \(7.053 = (7 \times 1) + (5 \times 0.01) + (3 \times 0.001)\), and the 5 is worth \(0.05\), not 5 and not \(0.5\). On STAAR it is a supporting standard next to two readiness standards (5.3E and 5.4B), but it is the place-value floor that 5.3E decimal placement stands on.

Those three travel together in a vault format because missions can specialize, then a boss mixes them the way a STAAR set does. A single “solve for \(x\)” worksheet cannot carry that arc.

## Classroom pitfalls that look like “we did equations”

**Padlock trivia as the whole review.** Bare facts behind a cartoon lock keep the room quiet. They do not practice 5.4B equation-writing, 5.3E decimal placement in context, or 5.2A expanded notation through thousandths. Save the fact warm-up for the first three minutes — do not let it eat the block.

**Solving without representing.** Teachers search “5.4B equations” because kids can get 86 markers but cannot pick the equation that produces it. If every item says “how many are left?” and none says “which equation,” half of 5.4B never gets rehearsed.

**“Just move the decimal two places.”** That rule works until it doesn’t. \(1.6 \times 3.25\) needs three decimal places in the whole-number path (\(16 \times 325 = 5200\), so \(5.200\)), and money answers like $3.15 vs $31.50 vs $315.00 are exactly where STAAR distractors live. Teach the place-value reason, then make kids reread what is being multiplied.

**Calculator on because “it’s just review.”** Calculators are not permitted on STAAR grades 3–7 mathematics unless a student is eligible for one as a designated support. If your review lets the device place the decimal, you are rehearsing the wrong test.

**Baby clipart for ten-year-olds.** Fifth graders can tell when a room was made for second graders. A mission look with cadets their age buys more buy-in than kawaii mascots.

## What a good Grade 5 number vault looks like

Keep setup light enough for one review block or a two-day split.

1. **Force-copy Google Slides + Present mode.** Kids play their own copy. You keep the teacher master. No editor-share chaos.
2. **Pairs with a talk job.** “Which equation matches the story? How many decimal places should the product have? All the bottles, or one crate?”
3. **Chips that build a short code.** Correct work earns progress. Wrong answers loop with a hint, not a lecture from you at every desk.
4. **Self-checking slides plus a paper recording sheet.** Pencil work keeps kids honest about the equation they chose and where they put the decimal.
5. **A mixed boss at the end.** 5.2A, 5.3E, and 5.4B in one finish — the closest thing to how STAAR stacks skills.

Place value → decimal products → two-step money → equations → mixed boss is a clean review arc.
