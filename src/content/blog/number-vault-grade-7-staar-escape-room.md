Every November, middle school math teachers start hunting for the same thing: a no-prep escape room for the last few days before winter break. When we looked at what turns up on TPT this week, the demand was easy to see. Review after review mentions using one "before winter break." The gap was easy to see, too. One of the most-reviewed "middle school" holiday escape rooms is mostly multi-digit multiplication and division. That's Grade 4–5 work with a seventh-grade label on it.

Kids love an escape room in December, and that's fine. The problem is wasting the class period. A seventh grader who spends 50 minutes on 3-digit times 2-digit hasn't practiced anything the spring test will ask. The same 50 minutes could cover rational numbers, rates, percents and two-step equations, with the same lock-and-chip fun.

Here's what a real Grade 7 review day should cover, the misses to watch for, and one game we built for it.

## The four standards that carry Grade 7 computation

TEA's Grade 7 Mathematics list puts these four in Strand 2, *Computations and Algebraic Relationships*. All four are **readiness standards** ([TEA, Grade 7 Mathematics Assessment, Eligible TEKS, revised August 2026](https://tea2.tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/sst-7-math-assessed-curriculum.pdf)). In plain words:

- **7.3B:** solve problems that add, subtract, multiply and divide rational numbers: negatives, fractions and decimals, often all in one story.
- **7.4A:** represent a constant rate of change as a table, a graph, words or an equation, including d = rt.
- **7.4D:** solve ratio, rate and percent problems, including multi-step percent increase and decrease and financial literacy (discounts, tax).
- **7.11A:** model and solve one-variable, two-step equations and inequalities.

The same TEA document says these content standards stay the same for the Student Success Tool, which TEA plans to start in fall 2027. Grade 7 math is also a no-calculator test unless a student is eligible for one as a designated support ([TEA STAAR Calculator Policy](https://tea2.tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/staar-calculator-policy.pdf)). Your review day should run pencil-only.

## The misses that cost points

**1. Sign slips in a multi-step story (7.3B).**
"The temperature at sunrise is −3.6°F. It rises 5.2 degrees by noon, then falls 4.1 degrees by evening." A student who loses track of the sign gets 5.7 or −12.9. The answer is −3.6 + 5.2 = 1.6, then 1.6 − 4.1 = −2.5°F. The fix is boring and it works: **write the sign of every step before you compute.** Then ask, "Should the answer be above or below zero?" before anyone picks a choice.

**2. Adding percents that should be stacked (7.4D).**
"A $60 jacket is 30% off. At checkout there's an extra 10% off the sale price." Many students add the percents, take 40% off, and get $36. But the second discount applies to the *new* price: 30% off gives $42, then 10% of $42 is $4.20, so the jacket costs $37.80. The question that fixes it is: **"What's the new price before the next percent?"** A quick follow-up shows it in reverse: $50 down 20% is $40, and $40 up 20% is $48, not $50. Kids remember that one.

**3. A rate table or graph that doesn't start at zero (7.4A).**
"A cyclist rides at a constant 14 km per hour." The matching table is t = 0, 1, 2, 3 with d = 0, 14, 28, 42, and the matching equation is d = 14t. The trap answers are built from d = t + 14 (15, 16, 17…) or a line that starts at (0, 14). Quick check: at t = 0, has she gone anywhere? No, so a constant rate passes through (0, 0). And d ÷ t should be 14 in every row.

**4. Writing the equation in the wrong order (7.11A).**
"Marco doubles his card collection, then gets 6 more. Now he has 50." That's 2n + 6 = 50, so 2n = 44 and n = 22. Students who write 2(n + 6) = 50 get 19, and that number will be sitting in the answer choices. Have them read the story in order and build the expression in the same order: *doubles* first, *then* adds 6.

Inequalities add one more step: the words. "Lena has $35 and saves $15 a week. How many weeks until she has at least $140?" *At least* means ≥, so 35 + 15w ≥ 140, then 15w ≥ 105, and w ≥ 7. Then substitute the boundary: 35 + 15 × 7 = 140. ✓

## What a good pre-break review day looks like

- **Grade-level items, not bigger arithmetic.** Negatives and fractions in context, stacked percents, rate tables, two-step equations.
- **Wrong choices that show real mistakes.** The "added the percents" price, the step-one answer, and the wrong-order equation should all be options, so a miss tells you something.
- **A hint, then another try.** A wrong tap should bring up a strategy, not the answer.
- **Paper backup.** A recording sheet keeps the work visible, and it saves the day if the Wi-Fi doesn't cooperate.
- **A finish line.** In December, a vault that opens and a certificate do more for focus than a worksheet does.

## After the game: a two-minute debrief

Collect the recording sheets and find the most-missed item. In Grade 7 it's usually a Percent Protocol item where a pair added the percents. Before break (or on the first day back), put one fresh stacked-discount story on the board. Have students write the new price after each step. That habit carries straight into the spring.

## The short version

A December escape room is a good idea. A Grade 4 escape room with a middle school label isn't. Spend the period on the four Grade 7 readiness standards that carry computation: rational numbers, constant rates, multi-step percents, and two-step equations and inequalities. Keep two questions on repeat: *What's the new price before the next percent?* and *What happens first in the story?*
