# Section 1: TIA Overview

## What Is the Teacher Incentive Allotment?

The **Teacher Incentive Allotment (TIA)** is a Texas state program (House Bill 3, 2019) that allows districts to provide **permanent salary increases** to teachers who are designated as **Recognized, Exemplary, or Master** based on their effectiveness.

Unlike bonuses that come and go, TIA designations are:
- **Portable** — they follow you to any participating district in Texas
- **Permanent** — as long as you maintain the designation (redesignation every 5 years)
- **Funded by the state** — districts receive additional allotment funding per designated teacher

## Designation Levels & Potential Salary Increases

| Designation | Description | Approximate Annual Increase* |
|-------------|-------------|------------------------------|
| **Recognized** | Consistently effective teacher with strong student outcomes | **$3,000 – $9,000** |
| **Exemplary** | Highly effective teacher with exceptional results | **$6,000 – $18,000** |
| **Master** | Among the most effective educators in the state | **$9,000 – $32,000** |

*\*Actual amounts vary by district and campus. Rural and high-needs campuses receive higher allotments.*

### How the Funding Works

The state calculates allotment amounts based on:
1. **Your designation level** (Recognized/Exemplary/Master)
2. **Your campus socioeconomic profile** (higher funding for high-needs campuses)
3. **Your district's approved local designation system (LDS)**

Teachers at **rural and high-needs campuses** can earn significantly more — the state incentivizes keeping excellent teachers where they're needed most.

## Who Is Eligible?

- **All** classroom teachers in Texas public schools
- Your district must have a **TEA-approved Local Designation System (LDS)**
- You must meet the criteria set by your district's system
- Both tested and non-tested subject teachers can participate

## How Long Does It Take?

Most districts collect data over one full school year. The typical timeline:
- **Year 1:** Data collection (you're doing this now!)
- **Year 2:** District submits data to TEA → You receive your designation
- **Ongoing:** Designation lasts 5 years, then you redesignate

# Section 2: The Two Pillars

## How TIA Designations Are Determined

Every TEA-approved Local Designation System must evaluate teachers on **two pillars**:

### 🏛️ Pillar 1: Teacher Observation (Qualitative)

This is based on your **T-TESS (Texas Teacher Evaluation and Support System)** observation scores or an equivalent approved rubric.

**What counts:**
- Formal observations by trained appraisers
- Walkthroughs (if your district includes them in LDS)
- T-TESS domain scores (Planning, Instruction, Learning Environment, Professional Practices & Responsibilities)
- Post-observation conference ratings

**Typical requirements by designation:**

| Designation | Typical T-TESS Requirement |
|-------------|---------------------------|
| Recognized | Average of "Accomplished" across observed domains |
| Exemplary | Average of "Accomplished" with some "Distinguished" |
| Master | Average of "Distinguished" across observed domains |

### 🏛️ Pillar 2: Student Growth (Quantitative)

This measures the **academic growth** of your students over time.

**For STAAR-tested subjects:**
- Student Growth Percentile (SGP) — how much your students grew compared to similar students statewide
- Individual student growth data from STAAR

**For non-STAAR subjects:**
- District-approved Student Learning Objectives (SLOs)
- Pre/post assessments with validated growth measures
- Portfolio-based evidence of student growth (district-specific)

**Typical requirements by designation:**

| Designation | Typical Growth Requirement |
|-------------|---------------------------|
| Recognized | Above-average student growth (55th+ percentile) |
| Exemplary | Well above average (60th–70th+ percentile) |
| Master | Top-tier student growth (75th+ percentile) |

### The Weight Split

TEA requires that **both pillars** factor into the designation. Most districts weight them:
- **Observation:** 30–50% of the overall score
- **Student Growth:** 50–70% of the overall score

> ⚠️ **Key Takeaway:** You cannot earn a designation on observation scores alone OR growth data alone. You need strong performance in BOTH areas.

# Section 3: Building Your Portfolio — Step by Step

## Your TIA Portfolio Roadmap

Even if your district doesn't require a formal "portfolio," building one helps you:
- Organize evidence for observation conversations
- Document student growth proactively
- Prepare for redesignation
- Build a professional artifact you'll use for years

### Step 1: Know Your District's LDS (August)

**Action Items:**
- [ ] Obtain a copy of your district's TEA-approved Local Designation System document
- [ ] Identify the specific criteria for each designation level
- [ ] Note the observation rubric being used (T-TESS or alternative)
- [ ] Understand how student growth is measured in your subject/grade
- [ ] Identify deadlines for data submission
- [ ] Ask your administrator: "What evidence would be most helpful for my TIA data?"

### Step 2: Establish Baseline Data (August–September)

**Action Items:**
- [ ] Administer pre-assessments for all units/courses
- [ ] Document pre-assessment data in a tracking system
- [ ] Set Student Learning Objectives (SLOs) if required
- [ ] Photograph/scan any relevant baseline student work samples
- [ ] Record beginning-of-year benchmark scores

### Step 3: Collect Evidence Continuously (September–April)

**Action Items:**
- [ ] Save lesson plans that show differentiation and rigor
- [ ] Document student growth data after each assessment
- [ ] Keep a log of professional development attended
- [ ] Save examples of student work showing growth (before/after)
- [ ] Document parent/family communication
- [ ] Track participation in PLCs, committees, mentoring
- [ ] Save observation feedback and your reflections

### Step 4: Prepare for Observations (Ongoing)

**Action Items:**
- [ ] Review T-TESS rubric before each observation window
- [ ] Plan lessons that demonstrate "Distinguished" indicators
- [ ] Prepare your classroom environment (word walls, data displays, student work)
- [ ] Have your pre-observation form completed thoroughly
- [ ] Practice your post-observation conference talking points

### Step 5: Compile Growth Data (April–May)

**Action Items:**
- [ ] Administer post-assessments
- [ ] Calculate student growth (percentage growth, point growth)
- [ ] Create data visualizations (charts, graphs)
- [ ] Identify growth by subgroup (ELL, SpEd, etc.)
- [ ] Write a narrative summary of student growth
- [ ] Prepare comparison data (your students vs. campus/district averages if available)

### Step 6: Assemble Your Portfolio (May–June)

**Action Items:**
- [ ] Organize all evidence by T-TESS domain
- [ ] Write reflective narratives for each domain
- [ ] Include student growth data with analysis
- [ ] Add professional development certificates
- [ ] Include letters of recommendation or peer feedback (optional)
- [ ] Submit any required documentation to your district

# Section 4: Evidence Collection Checklists

## Organized by T-TESS Domain

Use these checklists to ensure you have evidence across all evaluated areas.

---

### Domain 1: Planning

*"The teacher designs clear, well-organized, sequential lessons that reflect best practice, align with standards, and are appropriate for diverse learners."*

**Evidence to Collect:**
- [ ] Unit plans showing alignment to TEKS
- [ ] Lesson plans with clear objectives, activities, and assessments
- [ ] Evidence of backward design (starting with the end assessment)
- [ ] Differentiated lesson plans (ELL modifications, SpEd accommodations, GT extensions)
- [ ] Plans that integrate technology meaningfully
- [ ] Cross-curricular connection plans
- [ ] Plans that incorporate student interests or real-world applications
- [ ] Evidence of using data to inform planning (e.g., re-teach plans based on assessment results)
- [ ] Intervention group plans
- [ ] Plans showing scaffolding for complex tasks
- [ ] Evidence of collaboration with co-teachers, paraprofessionals, or specialists

**Distinguished Indicators to Target:**
- Plans are adjusted based on continuous analysis of student data
- Lessons connect learning across disciplines
- Students are involved in planning their own learning paths

---

### Domain 2: Instruction

*"The teacher uses a variety of instructional strategies to ensure student engagement and learning."*

**Evidence to Collect:**
- [ ] Video clips of instruction (if allowed by district)
- [ ] Photographs of students engaged in learning activities
- [ ] Examples of higher-order questioning strategies used
- [ ] Student discussion protocols or Socratic seminar documentation
- [ ] Evidence of formative assessment during instruction (exit tickets, quick checks)
- [ ] Documentation of varied grouping strategies (whole group, small group, partners, independent)
- [ ] Technology integration evidence
- [ ] Evidence of real-world connections in instruction
- [ ] Student feedback on instruction (surveys, reflections)
- [ ] Examples of how you adjusted instruction mid-lesson based on student responses
- [ ] Documentation of academic vocabulary instruction

**Distinguished Indicators to Target:**
- Students take ownership of their learning
- Instruction seamlessly adjusts based on real-time data
- Students make connections across content areas independently

---

### Domain 3: Learning Environment

*"The teacher creates a classroom environment that is safe, organized, and focused on learning."*

**Evidence to Collect:**
- [ ] Photographs of classroom setup (seating arrangement, learning stations)
- [ ] Photos of word walls, anchor charts, data walls
- [ ] Classroom management plan/procedures document
- [ ] Evidence of positive behavior systems
- [ ] Documentation of classroom routines and transitions
- [ ] Student voice/choice evidence (student-led activities, choice boards)
- [ ] Photos or documentation of student work displays
- [ ] Evidence of a culturally responsive classroom environment
- [ ] Morning meeting or community-building activity plans
- [ ] Seating arrangement rationale (flexible seating, strategic grouping)
- [ ] Safety protocols documentation

**Distinguished Indicators to Target:**
- Students self-manage and hold each other accountable
- The classroom functions as a student-led learning community
- Environment reflects student ownership (student-created displays, rules)

---

### Domain 4: Professional Practices & Responsibilities

*"The teacher demonstrates the standards of the profession and contributes to the school community."*

**Evidence to Collect:**
- [ ] Professional development certificates and hours log
- [ ] PLC participation documentation (agendas, minutes, your contributions)
- [ ] Mentoring activities (formal or informal)
- [ ] Campus/district committee participation
- [ ] Parent/family communication log (emails, newsletters, conference notes)
- [ ] Participation in school events beyond the classroom
- [ ] Professional organization memberships
- [ ] Conference presentations or published work
- [ ] Peer observation notes (observing others or being observed)
- [ ] Self-reflection journals or documentation
- [ ] Evidence of implementing feedback from observations
- [ ] Documentation of advocating for students (ARD meetings, 504 meetings, etc.)
- [ ] Professional goal-setting documentation
- [ ] National Board Certification progress (if applicable)

**Distinguished Indicators to Target:**
- You lead professional learning for others
- You actively mentor and develop other teachers
- You contribute to school/district improvement initiatives

# Section 8: Common Mistakes to Avoid

## 🚫 Mistake #1: Waiting Until Spring to Collect Evidence

**Why it's a problem:** By April, you've forgotten the great lesson you taught in October. Observations only capture snapshots — you need documentation across the full year.

**What to do instead:** Set a recurring calendar reminder every Friday: "Save 1 piece of TIA evidence." Even a quick phone photo of student work or a screenshot of your lesson plan counts.

---

## 🚫 Mistake #2: Only Focusing on Observations

**Why it's a problem:** Observations (Pillar 1) are only 30-50% of your TIA score. Student growth (Pillar 2) is the MAJORITY. A perfect observation score with mediocre growth data won't get you a designation.

**What to do instead:** Invest equal or more energy into tracking and improving student growth data. Use a data tracker (like our Student Growth Tracking Template!), analyze results after every assessment, and adjust instruction based on what you find.

---

## 🚫 Mistake #3: Not Understanding Your District's Specific LDS

**Why it's a problem:** Every district's Local Designation System is different. What counts for TIA in Dallas ISD may not count in Katy ISD. If you're collecting the wrong evidence or using the wrong growth measures, it won't matter how hard you work.

**What to do instead:** Get your district's LDS document in August. Read it. Highlight the specific criteria. Ask your administrator or HR department if anything is unclear. Don't assume — verify.

---

## 🚫 Mistake #4: Ignoring Subgroup Data

**Why it's a problem:** Growth for ALL students matters, including ELL, SpEd, and At-Risk subgroups. If your class average looks great but your ELL students aren't growing, that's a gap that will show up in your data.

**What to do instead:** Disaggregate your data by subgroup regularly. Celebrate subgroup wins. Address subgroup gaps proactively. Document the interventions you're providing for each subgroup.

---

## 🚫 Mistake #5: Not Pre-Assessing

**Why it's a problem:** You can't show growth without a starting point. If you skip the pre-assessment, you have no baseline data — and no way to prove your students grew.

**What to do instead:** Give a pre-assessment at the beginning of every unit AND at the beginning of the year. It doesn't have to be long — even 10 questions that mirror the post-assessment will work. Save the data!

---

## 🚫 Mistake #6: Teaching to the Middle

**Why it's a problem:** Growth measures reward moving ALL students, not just the ones in the middle. If your highest students plateau because they're bored and your lowest students don't grow because they need more support, your growth numbers suffer.

**What to do instead:** Differentiate intentionally. Document your differentiation plans. Show how you challenge GT students with extensions and support struggling students with scaffolding.

---

## 🚫 Mistake #7: Treating the Post-Observation Conference as a Formality

**Why it's a problem:** The post-conference is where your appraiser finalizes your T-TESS scores. If you just nod and say "That went well," you're missing a chance to advocate for a higher rating.

**What to do instead:** Come prepared with:
- Specific evidence of what went well (tie it to T-TESS indicators)
- Context for anything that didn't go perfectly
- Questions about how to improve
- A growth-oriented mindset (appraisers LOVE teachers who reflect authentically)

---

## 🚫 Mistake #8: Not Communicating Your TIA Goals to Your Appraiser

**Why it's a problem:** Your appraiser may not know you're pursuing a TIA designation unless you tell them. They can't support you if they don't know what you're working toward.

**What to do instead:** Have an early-year conversation: "I'm working toward a [Recognized/Exemplary/Master] designation. Can we discuss what I should focus on?" Most appraisers want to help — but they need to know you're interested.

---

## 🚫 Mistake #9: Messy or Disorganized Evidence

**Why it's a problem:** Having evidence is only half the battle. If it's scattered across three apps, two binders, and your camera roll, you won't be able to find what you need when you need it.

**What to do instead:** Use ONE system. We recommend a Google Drive folder with subfolders for each T-TESS domain. Save files with clear naming conventions (e.g., "D2_Instruction_LessonPlan_Oct15.pdf").

---

## 🚫 Mistake #10: Giving Up After Year One

**Why it's a problem:** Many teachers don't get their desired designation the first year. Data collection is a skill that improves with practice. The first year teaches you what to do differently.

**What to do instead:** If you don't get the designation you wanted, ask for feedback. What was the gap? Was it growth data or observation scores? Use that information to target your efforts next year. Most successful Master-designated teachers didn't get there on the first try.
