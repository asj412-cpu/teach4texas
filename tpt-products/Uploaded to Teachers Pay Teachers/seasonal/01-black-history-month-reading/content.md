## Passage 1: Barbara Jordan — The Voice of Texas

### Reading Passage (Grade 3-5, Lexile 780-850)

Have you ever heard a voice so powerful that it made an entire room go silent? That was the voice of Barbara Charline Jordan.

Barbara was born on February 21, 1936, in Houston, Texas. She grew up in the Fifth Ward, one of Houston's oldest Black neighborhoods. Her family didn't have much money, but they had something even more valuable — a deep belief in education and hard work.

As a young girl, Barbara discovered she had a gift. When she spoke, people listened. She joined her high school debate team at Phillis Wheatley High School and quickly became one of the best speakers in the state. Barbara won debate competitions against students from all over Texas — even schools that had more resources and funding.

After high school, Barbara attended Texas Southern University, where she continued to sharpen her speaking skills. She then went to Boston University Law School and became a lawyer. But Barbara had bigger dreams. She wanted to change the laws that treated Black people unfairly.

In 1966, Barbara Jordan made history. She became the first Black woman ever elected to the Texas Senate. At the time, many people in government didn't think a Black woman belonged there. Barbara proved them wrong — not with anger, but with intelligence, preparation, and that incredible voice.

But Barbara wasn't finished making history. In 1972, she was elected to the United States Congress, becoming one of the first Black women from the South to serve there. She worked on laws to protect the rights of all Americans, especially those who had been left out.

One of her most famous moments came in 1974 during the Watergate hearings. The whole nation watched on television as Barbara spoke about the Constitution. Her words were so moving and so clear that people across America — no matter their race or political party — were deeply impressed.

Barbara Jordan once said, "What the people want is very simple. They want an America as good as its promise." She spent her entire life trying to make that happen.

Barbara passed away in 1996, but her legacy lives on. The Barbara Jordan Terminal at Austin-Bergstrom International Airport is named in her honor. She showed the world that a girl from Houston's Fifth Ward could change the course of American history.

## Passage 2: Bessie Coleman — Queen Bess of the Skies

### Reading Passage (Grade 3-5, Lexile 760-830)

Imagine looking up at the sky and dreaming of flying — but being told you could never do it because of the color of your skin. That's exactly what happened to Bessie Coleman, and it only made her more determined.

Bessie was born on January 26, 1892, in Atlanta, Texas — a small town in the northeast corner of the Lone Star State. She was the tenth of thirteen children. Her family were sharecroppers, which meant they farmed someone else's land and gave most of what they grew to the landowner. Life was hard, but Bessie was tough.

As a child, Bessie walked four miles to her one-room schoolhouse every day. She loved reading and was especially good at math. She dreamed of doing something important with her life — something no one expected from a young Black girl in rural Texas.

When Bessie grew up, she moved to Chicago to live with her brothers. There, she heard stories from soldiers returning from World War I about flying airplanes. Bessie was fascinated. She decided right then that she would become a pilot.

But there was a problem. No flight school in America would accept a Black woman. Not a single one. Most people would have given up, but Bessie had another idea. She learned French and applied to flight schools in France, where the rules were different.

In 1921, Bessie Coleman earned her international pilot's license from the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale in France. She became the first Black woman — and the first Native American woman (her mother was part Cherokee) — to earn a pilot's license anywhere in the world.

Bessie returned to America as a celebrity. Newspapers called her "Queen Bess" and "Brave Bessie." She performed daring tricks at air shows, doing loops, dives, and figure-eights that thrilled huge crowds. But Bessie had a rule: she refused to perform at any event that segregated Black and white audience members. If everyone couldn't enter through the same gate, she wouldn't fly.

Bessie dreamed of opening a flight school for Black Americans, but tragically, she died in a plane accident in 1926 at just 34 years old. She never got to open her school, but she inspired generations of Black pilots who came after her.

Today, every year on the anniversary of her death, Black pilots fly over Bessie's grave in Lincoln Cemetery in Chicago and drop flowers from their planes. The little girl from Atlanta, Texas changed aviation history forever.

## Passage 3: Dr. Heman Marion Sweatt — The Man Who Opened the Door

### Reading Passage (Grade 3-5, Lexile 770-840)

Sometimes, changing history starts with doing something that seems simple — like applying to school. That's how Heman Marion Sweatt changed Texas forever.

Heman was born in 1912 in Houston, Texas. He grew up in a family that believed deeply in education. His father was a school principal, and education was treated as the most important thing in the Sweatt household.

After graduating from Wiley College, a historically Black college in Marshall, Texas, Heman worked as a mail carrier. But he had a bigger dream: he wanted to become a lawyer. In 1946, he applied to the University of Texas School of Law in Austin.

There was just one problem. The University of Texas did not allow Black students. Texas, like many southern states, had laws that kept Black people and white people in separate schools. This was called segregation, and it was legal at the time.

When the university rejected Heman because of his race, he didn't walk away quietly. With the help of the NAACP (National Association for the Advancement of Colored People), Heman sued the state of Texas. His case, called *Sweatt v. Painter*, went all the way to the United States Supreme Court — the highest court in the country.

Texas tried to solve the problem by quickly creating a separate law school just for Black students. But the new school was tiny and had almost no books, no real campus, and very few professors. It was nowhere close to equal to the University of Texas.

In 1950, the Supreme Court made its decision. The justices ruled that the separate law school was NOT equal, and that Heman Sweatt had the right to attend the University of Texas School of Law. It was a groundbreaking victory.

Heman's case was one of the most important steps toward ending segregation in American education. Just four years later, the Supreme Court used similar reasoning in the famous *Brown v. Board of Education* case, which ended school segregation across the entire country.

Heman Marion Sweatt faced threats and harassment when he enrolled at UT Law. The pressure eventually affected his health, and he left the law school. But his courage opened the door for thousands of Black students who came after him. Today, a directory at the UT School of Law bears his name, and he is remembered as one of the most important civil rights heroes in Texas history.

## Passage 4: Congresswoman Eddie Bernice Johnson — Science, Service, and Texas

### Reading Passage (Grade 3-5, Lexile 780-850)

What if someone told you that a nurse from Waco, Texas would one day lead the most powerful science committee in the United States government? That's the true story of Eddie Bernice Johnson.

Eddie Bernice Johnson was born on December 3, 1935, in Waco, Texas. Growing up in segregated Texas, she learned early that opportunities for Black women were limited — but that didn't stop her from dreaming big.

Eddie Bernice became a registered nurse and earned her nursing diploma from Holy Cross Central School of Nursing. She later earned a bachelor's degree from Texas Christian University and a master's degree in public administration from Southern Methodist University. Education was her pathway to creating change.

In 1972, Eddie Bernice Johnson was elected to the Texas House of Representatives, becoming the first Black woman from Dallas to serve in the Texas legislature. She immediately got to work, focusing on healthcare and helping people in her community get access to doctors and hospitals.

But Eddie Bernice didn't stop there. In 1992, she was elected to the United States Congress, representing Dallas, Texas. She would go on to serve for 30 years — one of the longest-serving members of Congress from Texas.

What made Eddie Bernice Johnson truly special was her love of science. In 2021, she became the chairwoman of the House Committee on Science, Space, and Technology — the first Black person and the first woman to ever lead this powerful committee. She helped make decisions about NASA, space exploration, and scientific research for the entire country.

From her position, she fought to make sure more women and minorities had opportunities in STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Math). She believed every child deserved the chance to become a scientist, engineer, or astronaut — no matter their background.

Eddie Bernice Johnson also helped bring the Inland Port and major economic development to southern Dallas, creating thousands of jobs for people in her community.

When she retired from Congress in 2023, she left behind a legacy of service, science, and breaking barriers. The girl from Waco proved that with education and determination, you can change the world — one law at a time.

## Passage 5: Matthew Gaines — From Slavery to the Texas Senate

### Reading Passage (Grade 3-5, Lexile 750-820)

Can you imagine being born into slavery, teaching yourself to read in secret, and then becoming a leader who helped write laws for an entire state? That's the remarkable story of Matthew Gaines.

Matthew Gaines was born into slavery in 1840 in Alexandria, Louisiana. As an enslaved person, he was not allowed to go to school. In most southern states, it was actually against the law to teach enslaved people to read or write. Slaveholders were afraid that education would make enslaved people want freedom even more.

But Matthew was not going to let anyone control his mind. In secret, he taught himself to read. He studied the Bible and became a powerful preacher. His words inspired the people around him, and he became a leader in his community even before he was free.

After the Civil War ended in 1865, Matthew Gaines was finally free. He settled in Washington County, Texas, near the town of Brenham. During this time, called Reconstruction, the United States was rebuilding, and for the first time, Black men were allowed to vote and hold public office.

In 1869, Matthew Gaines was elected to the Texas State Senate. He was one of only two Black senators in Texas at the time. Despite facing racism and opposition from many white lawmakers, Matthew fought hard for the things he believed in.

His biggest accomplishment was fighting for public education. Matthew Gaines helped create Texas's first public school system. He believed that education should be available to ALL children — Black and white, rich and poor. Before his work, many children in Texas had no school to attend at all.

Matthew also fought against a system called "convict leasing," where prisoners (who were often Black men arrested on minor charges) were forced to work for free, almost like slavery all over again. He spoke out against this injustice when few others would.

Unfortunately, Matthew Gaines's time in the Senate was cut short. His political opponents worked to remove him from office, and Reconstruction-era progress for Black Texans was rolled back. Many of the rights Black people had gained were taken away through new laws and intimidation.

Matthew Gaines died in 1900, and for many years, his story was nearly forgotten. But in 2021, a statue of Matthew Gaines was unveiled on the campus of Texas A&M University in College Station, near where he once lived and served. His story is now being told again — a story of courage, education, and the belief that all people deserve equal rights.

- **Reconstruction** (noun) — the period (1865-1877) when the U.S. was rebuilding after the Civil War
- **convict leasing** (noun) — a system where prisoners were rented out to work for private companies
- **unveiled** (verb) — shown to the public for the first time
