The last full week before winter break is the loud one. Assemblies, parties, and an early-release day eat the schedule, and the easy move is a coloring page with a paper-chain border.

Grade 4 STAAR math is still on the calendar. Three readiness standards fit a December block: comparing fractions with different numerators and denominators, perimeter and area of rectangles, and representing data on a frequency table, a dot plot, or a stem-and-leaf plot. Wrapping paper has length and width. Cookies split into fractions. A toy count becomes a plot. The week can keep that story and still give you something to look at afterward.

## The three TEKS that fit a December block

Here they are in classroom language. TEA's Grade 4 mathematics assessed curriculum labels each one a readiness standard ([TEA, Grade 4 Mathematics Assessment, Eligible TEKS, revised August 2026](https://tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/sst-4-math-assessed-curriculum.pdf)). The same document says these content standards stay the same for the Student Success Tool, which TEA plans to start in fall 2027.

**TEKS 4.3D** is comparing two fractions with different numerators and different denominators and recording the comparison with >, =, or <. Put this on the board before anyone opens a mission: which is greater, 5/8 or 3/4? Both common shortcuts land on 5/8. The numerator 5 is bigger than 3, and the denominator 8 is bigger than 4. Neither shortcut is the comparison. 3/4 is 6/8, so 3/4 is greater. A number line, or a drawn model of the same whole, makes that visible before a student chooses the larger-looking numbers.

**TEKS 4.5D** is solving problems about perimeter and area of rectangles whose dimensions are whole numbers. Have kids say the unit out loud: perimeter in inches, area in square inches. A sheet of wrapping paper that is 8 inches by 5 inches has a perimeter of 26 inches and an area of 40 square inches. Adding 8 + 5 and calling it area, or multiplying and calling it the perimeter, is the usual miss. The next miss is a missing side. If the perimeter is 28 inches and the length is 9 inches, the width is 5 inches, because half of 28 is 14 and 14 − 9 = 5. The shortcut 28 − 9 = 19 still includes the other length and both widths, so 19 is not the missing side. A two-step problem then asks for the area of that 9-by-5 rectangle, which is 45 square inches. Students who stop after finding the width choose 5 and never finish the area.

**TEKS 4.9A** is representing data on a frequency table, dot plot, or stem-and-leaf plot marked with whole numbers and fractions. This is the display standard. Solving one- and two-step problems from those displays is **TEKS 4.9B**, and TEA labels 4.9B a supporting standard in the same document. A December rep for 4.9A should include a stem-and-leaf plot and at least one display marked with a fraction, such as a dot plot of ribbon lengths in halves. A page of whole-number tallies never reaches that part of the standard.

## Where December math usually goes sideways

**A coloring page as the math block.** The room gets quiet. None of the three readiness standards gets a rep.

**Fraction comparison with no model.** Cross-multiplying can produce a correct symbol and still leave a student stuck when the item shows a number line. Put the model on the board first.

**One label for perimeter and area.** Ask for the unit before the number. A fence problem that comes back in square inches was an area move on a perimeter story.

**Data with no fraction and no stem-and-leaf.** 4.9A names three displays — a frequency table, a dot plot, and a stem-and-leaf plot — and it marks them with whole numbers and fractions. If the page never uses a fraction or a stem-and-leaf, the standard is only partly on the table.

**A five-day unit in a two-day week.** You need one class period, or a clean split across two days. Scenes that specialize, then a mixed finish, fit that better than a new mini-unit.

## What a useful Grade 4 Christmas escape room does

1. **Uses word problems and drawn models.** Rectangle, number-line, and frequency-table items should show the model, so kids practice reading the way a STAAR item is built.
2. **Names the TEKS.** 4.3D, 4.5D, and 4.9A, where you can point to them in a PLC.
3. **Self-checks.** Google Slides in Present mode should loop a wrong answer back with a hint. You cannot be the only answer key in a room of excited fourth graders.
4. **Has a paper trail.** A recording sheet shows who worked the item and who clicked through.
5. **Fits the week you actually have.** About 45 to 60 minutes, in pairs, with no calculator. STAAR does not permit a calculator on the grades 3–7 mathematics assessments unless a student is eligible for one as a designated support ([TEA STAAR Calculator Policy](https://tea2.tea.texas.gov/data-reports/staar/staar-calculator-policy.pdf)).

## Closing

December can be loud and still move three readiness standards. Keep the workshop story, use a model for the fraction comparison, make kids name the unit on perimeter and area, and put a fraction on the data display. Pick an activity that fits in one class period — or a clean two-day split — and leaves you a recording sheet. Your fourth graders will still go home talking about the workshop. They will have done the math that opens it.
